Proposition: States with fewer abortion clinics tend to have lower abortion rates.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Measuring the rate by where the procedure took place (-1.0). The y-axis counts abortions performed in each state, divided by that state's resident women. Residents who travel out of state are counted in the state they travel to, so low-clinic states look lower and high-clinic states look higher. After feedback on our checkpoint, we added the axis wording “performed in-state” to specify how the rate is counted. We first scored this decision -1.5 and now score it -1.0, because the label discloses the definition. What worked: we choose the wording that doesn't mention travel between states and use technical terms instead of explain the definition in detail, careless readers may still be fooled by it . What it did not do well: in our first version the definition was not specified at all, and the TA flagged it, so we had to add the label, which makes the technique easier to spot. The alternative was the residence-based rate, which counts abortions obtained by a state's residents wherever they were performed (the measure our opposing chart uses). We rejected it because it adds back out-of-state procedures for low-clinic states, which works against the story this chart is meant to tell.
  • Showing a rate per 1,000 women and no totals (-1.0). Dividing by resident women is a normal way to compare states of different sizes, but here it makes the number read as how many of that state's own women have abortions. New Jersey and New York sit near the top of the axis, and that is naturally read as residents' behavior rather than people coming in from other states. We deliberately do not show total counts or any comparison with residents, which would invite the question of where those procedures come from. What worked: the scatter is clean and comparable across states, and it keeps attention on the clinic-access trend instead of on state-to-state differences in procedure counts. What did not: a reader who wonders why a few states are so far above the rest gets no answer from the chart, and we cannot fully remove that question. The alternative was to plot totals, but then population would dominate and the clinic-access trend would be harder to see.
  • Flipping the x-axis so more clinic access is on the right (+1.0). Our first version plotted the percentage of women with no clinic in their county, so the trend sloped downward and readers had to reverse “more is worse” in their heads. Following feedback on our checkpoint, we switched to the percentage of women living in a county with a clinic, so the trend line slopes upward and reads naturally as “more access, higher rate,” with color-coded direction labels to make the axis explicit. What worked: the pattern is immediate and the direction labels remove ambiguity. What did not: the original orientation confused readers, which is why the TA flagged it, and the new measure is still a proxy for the proposition's “number of clinics,” which the chart does not point out. The alternative was keeping the original orientation with annotation arrows, which we found more confusing.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Selecting states with the largest differences (-2). We selected ten states with five or fewer abortion clinics that had the largest positive differences between their residence-based and occurrence-based abortion rates. This made the differences stand out and helped support our argument. What worked was that the selected states showed a clear gap between the two rates. However, we left out states with smaller or reversed differences, which could make the pattern seem more common than it actually is. An alternative would have been to include all states with five or fewer clinics to give a more complete picture. We gave this a -2 because selecting only the states with the largest positive differences makes the graph less representative of the full dataset.
  • Comparing two different abortion rates (+1). We compared abortions obtained by state residents with abortions performed within each state. These rates can differ because some people travel to other states for abortions. Showing both helps readers understand why the number of abortions performed in a state doesn't necessarily reflect how many abortions its residents obtain. What worked was that readers could compare the two measurements directly. However, the graph doesn't show exactly how many people traveled between states, so we can't tell the full reason for the differences from this graph alone. An alternative would have been to show only the residence-based rates, but including both gives readers more context. We gave this a +1 because showing both measurements makes the comparison more informative.
  • Using contrasting colors (-1). We used blue for residence-based abortion rates and light gray for occurrence-based rates. The colors make the two measurements easy to tell apart, and the blue bars stand out more. This helps draw attention to the residence-based rates, which support our argument. However, the gray bars are less noticeable, so the comparison isn't completely balanced. We could have used two colors with similar emphasis to give both measurements equal attention. We gave this a -1 because the colors put more attention on one measurement than the other.

Final Reflection

Overall, making our visualizations persuasive was relatively straightforward, as it involved focusing on a clear narrative, and we were surprised by how much the same data could be reframed without changing the underlying values to create this narrative. The difficult part was balancing that persuasive narrative with accurate interpretation from the audience. We noticed that small design choices such as axis direction and wording, significantly influenced how readers interpreted the data. Taking into account this feedback required us to balance transparency of the data with the intended narrative.

Given these learnings, we now define “ethical analysis and visualization” as the transparent and responsible presentation of data with the intent to provide value, recognizing that selectively emphasizing trends is not inherently unethical, but that even technically accurate information can become misleading when its framing encourages conclusions unsupported by the evidence. For example, using in-state procedures rather than residents’ procedures could create a misleading impression of residents’ behavior.

Some bounds we can draw on “acceptable” persuasive choices are highlighting legitimate patterns while preserving the conclusion readers are likely to draw. While, on the other hand, a “misleading” persuasive choice exploits ambiguity and not showing all the important context in order to encourage an unsupported interpretation by the overall data.
